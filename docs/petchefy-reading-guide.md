# A clearer way to work together at Petchefy

For Andres, Jeff, and Nan • Prepared with Daniel's help • October 8, 2026

## Why this is worth reading

Working in a small company means covering a lot of ground. People help wherever they can, responsibilities overlap, and agreements often happen in conversation. That flexibility is useful until the team can no longer tell who owns a result or whether a promise has been completed.

Daniel's starting concern is that responsibilities and follow-through at Petchefy need clarification. This guide treats that as a problem to examine together. It is not an assessment of any individual's effort, ability, or intent.

The opportunity is to make everyday work less frustrating. Each person should know what they own, what they can decide, what help they can expect, and how the team will judge whether the work is done. Someone asking for an update should be referring to an agreement everyone understands.

*Traction* is useful here because it connects role clarity to a regular way of checking commitments. It also asks whether a person actually wants and can carry the responsibility. Better tools alone cannot answer that question.

## What to do with the ideas

### Agree on responsibilities before attaching names

List the recurring work Petchefy actually needs. Possible areas might include customer acquisition, customer support, delivery, product work, and finances. These are examples to prompt discussion, not claims about Petchefy's structure.

For each area, describe a few concrete responsibilities and the result expected. Then agree on one accountable owner. Other people can contribute; the owner makes sure the work progresses and raises obstacles early. A three-person team can have more than three roles, but it cannot pretend everyone has unlimited capacity.

Also record what the owner can decide independently and which decisions need agreement. Responsibility without enough authority creates another source of delay.

### Check understanding, willingness, and capacity separately

Ask each person to describe the proposed role in their own words. Ask whether they want the responsibility and what would make delivery difficult. Discuss workload and available support before interpreting a missed commitment as a lack of effort.

AI belongs in that conversation where it can reduce a real burden. For example, it might help draft a response or summarize information. Someone still needs to check the output, make the decision, and complete the work. If the obstacle is unwillingness to own the result, adding another tool leaves that obstacle unresolved.

### Make commitments small enough to verify

For each commitment, record the owner, the due date, and what completion means. Avoid descriptions such as “work on marketing” that cannot be checked fairly. An illustrative alternative is “prepare a campaign brief with the audience, offer, budget, and launch date for team review by Friday.” Choose Petchefy's actual commitments together.

Keep the first week's list short. A realistic commitment completed on time teaches more than an ambitious list that everyone quietly abandons.

### Review the agreement every week

Use a consistent weekly meeting to check commitments, surface obstacles, and decide the next actions. A missed commitment should lead to a specific discussion about the cause and the next step. If the same problem continues after expectations and support are clear, discuss changing the role, workload, or arrangement. Repeatedly moving a deadline without discussing the cause provides little accountability.

For this small team, the suggested starting point is a 25-minute check-in. That is a Petchefy adaptation, not the book's full 90-minute Level 10 meeting format.

## The first conversation: 45 minutes

| Time | Discuss | Leave with |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–10 minutes | What work is getting missed, duplicated, or repeatedly chased? Use recent examples without guessing at motives | A shared list of concrete problems |
| 10–25 minutes | What recurring responsibilities does the business need, and who can own them? | A first version of the ownership table |
| 25–35 minutes | Does each owner understand and want the role, have capacity, and have enough authority? | Explicit agreement or an unresolved issue with someone responsible for resolving it |
| 35–45 minutes | What will each person finish before the next check-in? | A short list of commitments and a recurring meeting time |

Use the [team working agreement](../templates/team-working-agreement.md) during the discussion. Include Daniel's responsibilities and boundaries if he will be participating in the work. Do not assume that helping the team makes him responsible for managing it.

## What progress should look like after four weeks

The team should be able to identify the owner of each agreed responsibility without debate. Weekly commitments should have visible outcomes, and obstacles should come up early enough for someone to help. Where delivery still fails, the team should be able to name the unresolved issue and decide what to change.

At the fourth check-in, review what helped and what created unnecessary work. Update the agreement to reflect what the team learned. The value of reading these pages is the conversation and the agreements they make possible.
